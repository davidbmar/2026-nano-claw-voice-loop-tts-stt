How the Voice Line Listens, Thinks, and Speaks

What actually happens between the moment you stop talking and the moment the assistant answers — including what the little sounds mean.

The sounds you'll hear

A soft two-note chime right after you finish a sentence means "I heard you." Your words were captured and are on their way to being understood.
A quiet clock tick, about twice a second, means "working on it" — your speech is being turned into text and the assistant is composing its answer. The ticking stops the instant the reply begins. If you ask the assistant "what's that ticking?", it knows.

The journey of one exchange

1
You speak; the line listens for you to finish. A voice-activity detector watches the audio energy and calls your turn complete after a short natural silence. You can interrupt the assistant at any time just by talking — it stops mid-sentence and listens. While you speak, local Whisper repeatedly checks the growing audio and keeps the words that consecutive passes agree on.
2
Chime — you were heard. The acknowledgment plays immediately, before any processing begins.
3
Ticking — finish the tail, then think. Most transcription happened while you were talking, on this machine. Whisper now resolves only the short unstable tail. The text goes to two language models racing each other: a local model gets a head start, and a cloud model joins moments later if the local one is slow. Whichever produces words first wins the turn; the other is cancelled.
4
Speaking begins before the thinking ends. As the winning model writes its reply, each completed sentence is immediately compiled for the ear and spoken — the assistant starts talking while it is still writing the rest. That compilation is deliberate: pauses land after sentences, commas, and dashes at natural lengths (with a little human-like variation), and things that read badly aloud — prices, dates, times, phone numbers, "No. 5" — are expanded into spoken words. Lists get spoken ordinals: "First, … Second, …".
5
A local voice says it. The words are rendered by the LuxTTS voice engine running on the same machine, and paced out to the phone network frame by frame.

Why it's built this way

Silence on a phone line reads as a dead call — the chime and tick exist so the line always tells you where it is. Sentence-by-sentence speaking exists because waiting for a full paragraph before saying anything makes every answer feel slow; streaming the first sentence while the rest is written recovers most of that time without sacrificing the deliberate pause-and-phrasing polish. And the two-model race means a slow local model never makes you wait more than a moment — while a cloud outage still leaves a working local voice.

For the curious

Every call records a timeline you can replay in the /calls review panel: what was said on both sides, which model actually wrote each reply, what the turn cost, and the precise timing of every chime, tick, and sentence. Engineering details live in the design docs: streaming prepared speech, and the thinking-cue and call-review designs alongside it.
